Dynamics of Government-Opposition Parliamentary Relations and Public Attitudes

March 2023 – July 2027

Conflict lies at the heart of all politics. Even the most minimalist definition of democracy entails competition between candidates for public support. However, cooperation is also a central and necessary part of politics as the process by which society arrives at joint decisions. Aggregating interests and gaining majority support is inherent to politics in a modern democracy and requires cooperation within and between parties. Political parties, as the main actors in modern democracies, are therefore in a constant need to strike a balance between competition and conflict on the one hand and cooperation and compromise on the other.

Increasing dealignment, realignment, and electoral volatility push parties in opposite directions. The choices political elites make in how to interact with each other, to what extent these interactions are defined by negativity and conflict or by positivity and cooperation, affect, in turn, how citizens view political actors, institutions, and the democratic regime at large. Hence, elite political interactions may have fundamental implications for the quality and stability of representative democracy, which is why studying these interactions, their determinants, and their consequences is of utmost importance and urgency.

In this project, I investigated the trade-offs between conflict and cooperation from both the parties’ and the voters’ perspectives. I aimed to answer two overarching questions. The first related to the determinants of party behaviour and sought to fill the gap in the literature regarding causes for the change in that behaviour. Therefore, the question was how changes in public opinion inputs – through polls and subnational electoral results – affect parties’ interactions in parliament. The second question pertained to the effects of said interactions on voters’ attitudes toward the government and the opposition parties. Answering both questions is akin to closing a theoretical circle: parties are theoretically assumed to act in parliament, largely because they expect voters to react to their actions in a certain way. I therefore studied not only whether parties indeed act in the manner the theory predicts them to but also whether voters indeed react as parties seem to expect them to.

To answer the research questions, I constructed a broad comparative dataset of 12 countries, spanning overall 235 years of parliamentary activity, combining information on legislative votes, parliamentary speeches, electoral results, and polling data for all parties in the respective parliaments. Furthermore, I combined this dataset with several existing comparative surveys such as the CSES, ESS and Eurobarometer. Finally, I conducted a cross-national survey experiment to identify the micro-mechanisms underlying voters’ responses to party activity.

Outsiders at the Ballot Box: Integration, Discrimination, and Voting Patterns of Muslims in Europe

October 2026 – September 2028

A well-documented fact is that Muslim citizens tend to vote for left-wing parties in greater proportion than non-Muslims. In Western Europe, the gap in the vote for left-wing parties exceeds 30 percentage points and is extremely stable. Interestingly, the gap endures despite Muslims’ integration, which is expected to militate against group voting. Nevertheless, research on the voting behaviour of Muslims in Europe is not yet sufficiently developed. The proposed project aims to fill this lacuna, asking why it is that Muslims continue to vote as a group, and what factors account for their leaning towards the left.

We propose a theoretical framework which explains Muslims’ voting preferences by taking account not only of their integration into the host society, but also of the social exclusion and discrimination to which they are regularly subjected as a group. We argue that exclusion moderates the effect of integration on the vote choice of Muslims, as it limits the scope of mobilisation opportunities and strengthens the salience of group identity and group interests in the voting calculus of the individual. Specifically, we contend that exclusion will have greater salience in the vote of Muslims who are more integrated into the host society than in the electoral choices of their co-ethnics who are less so.

The empirical analysis will rely on online survey experiments and naturalistic quasi-experiments in Germany, Denmark, France and the UK. The experimental design of the surveys will expose Muslims to various manifestations of exclusion and thus will allow us to establish causality and identify mechanisms involved in the dynamic investigated.

Methodologically, experimental data will inform a causal account of the political behaviour of a growing electoral group in Europe. Theoretically, our project will shed light on a phenomenon that has already substantively affected the electoral landscape in Europe and has the potential to reshape it altogether by rendering ethnic and religious identity a crucial dimension of party competition.

The electoral consequences of party competition for the prime ministership

This project will begin in 2027.

Prime ministers (PMs) hold a uniquely influential position in parliamentary democracy, the prevalent regime type in Europe and most established democracies. This is especially true in recent decades, with increasing political personalisation and the increasing centralisation of power and resources in the hands of the PM and his or her office. Several PMs have also played a major role in the democratic backsliding of their countries, highlighting the social and real-world importance of studying the role of the PM in a wider range of political aspects.

Given the importance of PMs, the competition over the prime ministership presumably affects voters’ behaviour. However, most works do not theorise nor analyse this effect. If prime ministers and their parties wield such influence once in office, why do we know so little about how a party’s ability to compete for this position affects its electoral appeal?

The proposed project will address this gap by studying the effect of the competition between parties striving for the role of formateur, the one to form and lead the next government.

The project will answer the following research questions:

  1. Which party- and leader-related features determine which parties are considered as viable formateur candidate at a given election?
  2. What are the micro-level mechanisms that incentivise citizens to perform formateur-directed voting?

I will employ a multi-pronged approach. First, I will analyse, using several approaches, what makes a party a potential PM party. Second, I will formulate a novel theory of formateur-directed voting that combines both macro- and micro-level factors and test it with a multifaceted experimental and survey design: a series of lab experiments to test the micro-level mechanisms of the theory in a controlled, abstract environment; and pre-election surveys and survey experiments at the German state and national levels to collect a large sample with varying electoral scenarios while keeping many other political factors constant.

Beyond filling a glaring lacuna in the otherwise rich literature on voting behaviour, this project will contribute to the wider discipline by formalising the concept of a potential prime minister’s party. The project will also contribute to an ongoing debate on the role of individual versus common perceptions of electoral competition. Finally, the project will offer a better grasp of voting behaviour and its determinants, which is crucial in these times of shifting voting patterns, increased volatility, increased political personalisation, and challenges to the resilience of many European democracies.
